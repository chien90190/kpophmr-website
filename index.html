01Overview

A scale mismatch turns camera motion into human drift.

The problem 01

We introduce the K-Pop stress test, broadcast dance videos with fast, large camera motion and dense multi-person choreography. Leading methods produce plausible per-frame meshes on them, yet their world-space trajectories drift far from the performers' stage positions.

The diagnosis 02

Our diagnosis shows that the camera and human estimates each remain self-consistent, and that the drift appears when they are combined at mismatched scales. Each is recovered at its own scale, so the mismatch can turn camera motion into human drift that grows with camera displacement.

The correction 03

A closed-form correction estimates this mismatch and applies one scale to all performers, removing the drift while keeping their relative positions in a shared world frame. It needs no retraining and works with existing backbones.

03Method

One closed-form scale to remove the drift.

World space composes a camera estimate with a camera-space human estimate. On the stress test each passes its own check, but neither can see a wrong scale shared by the whole clip. The drift enters where the two are combined.

Diagnosis and correction

Two estimates of the same camera motion.

Visual odometry gives the camera's motion, ΔT. The meshes give a second estimate, ΔT', because each performer's position relative to the camera changes as it moves. The two should agree, but their scales can differ: ΔT' = sΔT. Once they are combined, a performer who is standing still appears to move by ΔŴ, a fraction of the camera's motion:

Δ W ^ ≈ ( 1 − s ) Δ T

Both ΔŴ and ΔT can be read off the pipeline, so every stretch where a performer stands still is one measurement of s. We pool these over performers p and frame pairs (i, j), each weighted by its contact confidence w. A least-squares fit then gives s in closed form:

s * = 1 − ∑ w p , i j Δ W ^ p , i j ⊤ Δ T i j ∑ w p , i j ‖ Δ T i j ‖ 2

Part 2 · 616 clips

Prior methods get either 2D alignment or world-space stability.

On the 616 in-the-wild clips, methods are scored by how stable the result is in world space and how well it still fits the video. TRAM and HAC fit the video best, since they never change a frame's prediction after placing it in the world. HAC sits furthest from the reference in the table above and skates 2.8× more than ours on the same backbones.

05Applications

Stable world space opens up new views of the performance.

In one stable world frame, the performance can be re-filmed with a new camera, even a real one from another broadcast.
